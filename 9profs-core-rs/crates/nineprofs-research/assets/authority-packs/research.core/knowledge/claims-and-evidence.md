# Claims and Evidence

## `claim-evidence-relationship`

A scientific claim should have an identifiable basis. The basis may include present-study results, cited literature, accepted background knowledge, explicit assumptions, logical derivation, qualitative material, documentary evidence, or theoretical reasoning.

## `claim-strength`

The strength of a claim should be proportional to the strength of its support. Stronger formulations such as proves, demonstrates, causes, confirms, establishes, or is effective require stronger support than formulations such as suggests, may indicate, is associated with, or was observed in.

## `observation-vs-inference`

A manuscript should distinguish what was observed from what is inferred. Inferential arguments are legitimate when the transition is justified rather than concealed.

## `evidence-relevance`

Evidence should support the particular claim for which it is used. A credible source may still be irrelevant to a specific assertion. Detailed verification of external sources belongs to evidence retrieval/citation validation.

## `absence-of-evidence`

Failure to observe something does not automatically establish its absence, especially when the study was not designed to detect it, measurement sensitivity is limited, or coverage is narrow.

## `unsupported-specificity`

Specific claims about mechanisms, motivations, causes, prevalence in unstudied populations, superiority, effectiveness, future consequences, or policy impact require commensurate support.

## `evidence-hierarchy-is-contextual`

`research.core` does not impose a universal hierarchy of designs or evidence types. Appropriate evidence depends on the question, discipline, object of inquiry, methodological tradition, design, and purpose of the claim.
